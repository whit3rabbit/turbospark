Coastal wetlands reduce flood damage through several mechanisms that work
together rather than in isolation.

The first is friction. Salt marsh grasses, mangrove roots, and seagrass
beds present an enormous amount of surface area to moving water. Each
stem and root extracts a small amount of energy from a passing wave, and
across a few hundred meters of vegetated shoreline those small losses
compound. Field measurements consistently show wave height falling by
half or more over that distance, which lowers the force arriving at any
seawall, road, or building behind the marsh.

The second is storage. A wetland is a shallow basin with a rough floor
and a high capacity to hold water temporarily. During a storm surge it
fills, and it drains slowly afterward through tidal creeks. That delay
matters more than the total volume: it spreads the arrival of water over
hours instead of minutes, so downstream channels carry a lower peak
discharge and are less likely to overtop their banks.

The third is elevation. Marshes trap sediment carried in by the tide,
and the accumulated organic matter from dead plant material adds to it.
A healthy marsh therefore builds upward over time, and in many places it
can keep pace with moderate rates of sea level rise. This is the
property that distinguishes a living shoreline from a fixed structure. A
concrete seawall built to a design height is at its most protective on
the day it is finished and becomes less adequate every year afterward. A
marsh maintained with adequate sediment supply does the opposite.

There are limits worth stating plainly. Wetlands attenuate waves and
surge, but they do not stop a surge whose depth greatly exceeds the
vegetation height, because the water above the canopy passes over it
with little resistance. Their protective value also depends on width,
and narrow fringing marshes squeezed between development and the water
provide much less benefit than the numbers from wide systems suggest.
Where subsidence is rapid or sediment has been cut off by upstream dams,
a marsh can drown in place and convert to open water, at which point the
protection disappears entirely.

The practical conclusion is that wetland conservation belongs in flood
risk planning as infrastructure, not as amenity. The relevant comparison
is not marsh against nothing, but marsh plus a smaller engineered
structure against a larger engineered structure alone, evaluated over
the decades during which both must keep working.
